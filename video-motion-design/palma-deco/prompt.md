# GTA 6

An original coastal illustration system in which a drawn contour opens onto another place. Give an object enough specificity to read in solid ink before putting scenery or ornament inside it. Keep the palette of the world outside the contour distinct from the light seen through it. Build new figures, architecture, ornament and typography for the subject supplied by the reader.

## Shape and light

Start with one dominant silhouette, one clean interior opening and at least one near edge that can cover the opening. Draw the weight-bearing side of a figure or structure first. Keep fine features such as the eye, lip of a window or joint of a hand outside the busiest patterned area. Separate deep shadow, local pigment, grazing light and paper. Preserve enough unmarked space for a contour to read at thumbnail size.

Choose one of the two treatments below for a piece. Both share the same masking and spatial rules. Their finishes remain distinct.

- **Deco light.** Set a matte slate stage in #1C212C. Raise a plaque in #242B3A, cut opposite corners, and use a restrained magenta-to-amber or pink-to-cyan edge. Crop scallops, interlocking arches or a tritone scene inside the silhouette. Reserve #F8F9FC for lettering; a small warm coral script accent can sit beside a heavier condensed word. Keep broad areas of slate free of bloom.
- **Tidal intaglio.** Cut the exterior in plum #221D35 or petrol #142C37. Light the internal scene with guava #EE8383, peach #FFCC97 and warm paper #FFF0CE. Draw three line scales: the structural contour, the break between two planes and a tapered engraving stroke. Hatch along the material. Keep paper tooth and broken ink fixed to the surface they describe. Reflections on water spread horizontally and break around objects.

Apply a single pattern such as a stepped fan, ogee or interlocking arch to a silhouette. Clip its strokes at the edge and keep the negative openings the color of the stage. One scene may move from one family to another as the camera enters the aperture. A small hot edge can carry the eye across the change.

## Figure construction and continuity

Choose a head angle before drawing the features. In a true profile the bridge, tip, lips and chin share one continuous outer contour. Show one eye and one brow; place the ear behind the jaw hinge. In a three-quarter pose carry the center line from brow to philtrum around the head volume, compress the far cheek and foreshorten the far eye. Keep the near eye, nostril, mouth and chin on that same rotation. The head sits on a neck that meets the collar. Use the same construction whenever the character returns, including the small portraits and the closing image.

Build the arm from shoulder, elbow and paired wrist edges. For a palm carrying an object, show the object resting across the curled fingers with a visible contact edge, a distinct opposing thumb and a forearm that meets the palm continuously. Foreshorten fingers along a common knuckle arc. At a small scale a joined finger silhouette with one or two separation cuts is clearer than a row of identical prongs. Keep the drawing order anatomically consistent as the hand moves behind and in front of the prop. Inspect the opening, maximum reach and release at full size and in the card crop. In silhouette alone the viewer should be able to tell which way the person is looking and what the hand is doing.

## Spatial behavior

Register the aperture, the scenery behind it and the closest obstruction on separate planes. Shift the nearest obstruction furthest and the horizon least. An offset must uncover something that was hidden. Keep a recognizable anchor on both sides of a transition so a change in scale feels like travel. Treat a thin light on one physical edge as an accent; allow it to end naturally where the edge turns away.

For a static application, show the occlusion with overlapping shapes, changing detail density and contact shadows. On an interactive surface keep the label fixed and move the inlay behind it. Use a visible focus ring and communicate state through position or form as well as hue. Under reduced motion, reveal the final arrangement immediately. On a narrow frame recompose around the aperture. Keep the clean reading zone and simplify small detail before reducing type.

# Palma Deco on film

Build an original animated coastal title sequence. Use the Deco light treatment of the accompanying visual system. The content brief supplies the entire story.

## Rendering the rules on film

Smooth, continuous `30 fps` camera and silhouette motion paired with `12 fps` stepped celluloid grain. Nothing pops in or vanishes on a single frame:

- **Five-stage emblem/silhouette ignition (`0.45 s–0.65 s`):** (1) bipartite neon perimeter traces around the path (`0–35%`), (2) deep plum/indigo body fills in (`25–55%`), (3) inner double-bevel stroke sharpens (`45–70%`), (4) internal Art Deco pattern or double-exposure scene illuminates via directional light sweep (`50–90%`), (5) crisp `2 px` rim highlight and floating label settle with a `4–6%` ease-out overshoot (`75–100%`).
- **Secondary life:** while held on screen, every clipped interior pattern or double-exposure landscape drifts slowly in parallax (`8–16 px/s` opposite to camera creep), neon rims breathe softly (`±8%` glow intensity at `0.4 Hz`), and characters blink (`0.12 s` every `2.6–3.4 s` on a seeded clock) with gentle `2–3 px` breathing motion.
- **Exit:** silhouettes dim their internal light first (`0.25 s`) while the perimeter rim recedes or sweeps away across a physical portal transition. Consecutive identical frames must stay `<= 15%` (target `< 4%`).

## Story, transitions, camera, sound and review

### Cast construction

The style does not know who is in the story. Anyone the brief describes becomes a **Palma Deco silhouette figure**:

- **Proportions & scale.** In main scenes, the figure in focus is at least one third of frame height (`>= 360 px` at `1080p`, ideally `420–560 px`) with a face height of at least `50 px`. With more than `4` figures, present `2` to `4` at a time in alternating left/right gallery compositions (keep each supplied task legible across the station and its close-up), and gather the full cast only at the reunion beat where no face is under `24 px` (excess items group under a large representative silhouette).
- **Layered silhouette construction.** Each figure has a clean outer vector contour (distinctive hairstyle, beard, collar, glasses, posture, or held prop) that reads instantly at thumbnail size. Inside the figure's `clip()` region sits either a themed Art Deco pattern or a moody duotone/tritone environment (coastal skyline, studio window, palms, horizon) that reflects their role, while the face/head carries crisp stylized graphic planes (2-tone graphic shadow + `#FFF3E0` or `#38E4F0` rim edge) so expressions (eyes, brows, mouth, head tilt) remain unmistakable.
- **Consistency & acting.** Keep the protagonist's silhouette, hair, beard/glasses, and signature accent hue identical from frame `0` to the final frame. Every gesture uses 3-part action (3–5 frame anticipation, overshoot, settle) and eyes track the active object or counter.

### A list of N things

Never assume `N`. Every list item becomes a clipped Deco medallion or chamfered card with its own distinct geometric pattern or silhouette icon:

- **N up to 8:** items ignite sequentially every `0.22–0.30 s` in an alternating left-right gallery or arc around the central hub, each tracing its neon rim before lighting its interior.
- **N from 9 to 24:** group items into `2` to `4` spacious Deco station cards (keep each supplied task legible across the station and its close-up) across sequential beats, holding `>= 1.8 s` per group so every icon and label reads cleanly.
- **N above 24:** introduce top-level groups in `4`-card showcases first, then pull back to a breathable Deco mosaic of well-spaced mini-plaques (`18 px` gutters, distinct icon per tile, live rim shimmer) paired with a rising sunburst progress chart and hero counter.

### Steps

Each step of the brief gets a physical material verb — *trace, ignite, mask-dive, recalibrate, align, bloom* — and a distinct lighting family (switching between Sunset Coral, Bayou Cyan, and Electric Night to mark narrative turns). Always carry one continuous element (the protagonist silhouette, a glowing connector rail, or the central crest) across each transition.

### Cause, error and fix

Cause travels as a luminous bipartite pulse along a drawn Deco rail. On an error or obstacle, the pulse rebounds in stuttering coral-pink back to the central hub, shifts `3` physical Deco equalizer dials or aperture rings into alignment, and fires a clean golden-cyan surge forward that locks the fix.

### Numbers and short words

Key numbers render at `>= 96 px` (`130–150 px` for the climax ratio/figure) as masked sunset-gradient numerals with a `2 px` white rim and soft magenta bloom. Keep on-screen text to concise labels and eyebrows — never walls of subtitle prose.

### Climax and ending

Drop the synth groove and ambient bed for `0.30 s` right before the climax hit while the central silhouette holds in deep plum backlighting with a razor-sharp rim. On the climax frame, ignite the full golden-hour double-exposure horizon inside the protagonist and centerpiece (`>= 360 px` tall) flanked symmetrically by active Deco plaques, lock the largest closing ratio/statement with a single sunburst flare, and hold the final hero frame for at least `1.2 s` with gentle grain, rim shimmer, and idle breathing.

### Pacing by duration

Frame `0` (`t = 0`) is ALREADY an active, legible hook: a lit silhouette emblem or protagonist framed by bipartite neon rim light on `#1C212C` slate — never a blank fade-in from black. Before drawing, write a beat table (start, end, lighting family, on-screen text, physical transition, and main sounds) so picture and audio share one exact clock.

## Transitions

Every scene change is a physical event of the Palma Deco world (`0.45 s–0.85 s`), hiding the swap at peak coverage:

1. **Silhouette Portal Dive (`0.55 s–0.75 s`):** The camera pushes smoothly (`log-scale zoom`, `ease.inOutCubic`) straight through the glowing interior or negative-space cutout of the active silhouette/emblem; at peak zoom the new lighting family fills the frame and pulls back to reveal the next composition.
2. **Neon Perimeter Sweep (`0.45 s–0.65 s`):** A diagonal bipartite light beam (magenta leading edge, amber or cyan trailing edge) wipes across the `#1C212C` stage, extinguishing the old silhouettes' internal light and tracing the contours of the incoming silhouettes in one continuous sweep.
3. **Parallax Collage Tunnel (`0.60 s–0.85 s`):** Four depth-stacked painted/Deco planes (foreground deep-foliage vignette, mid-plane Deco arches, rear collage decals, and focal center) rush past the camera at `1.8x / 1.2x / 0.7x / 0.4x` parallax speeds, emerging cleanly into the next matte slate gallery beat.
4. **Horizontal Star-Burst Compression (`0.40 s`):** Reserved strictly for a single time-jump cut (at most once per film): active neon elements collapse horizontally into a bright `6 px` light bar at center and snap open into the new beat.

## Camera

Two to five deliberate camera moves per 15 s (`ease.inOutCubic`, log-scale zoom between `0.86x` and `1.38x`, with up to `2.4x` during a Silhouette Portal Dive). Add a subtle continuous ambient drift (`7–9 px/s`) so no frame is ever frozen. Keep world-space decals and Art Deco textures anchored to their planes, compensate stroke widths by `1 / Math.sqrt(zoom)`, and counter-scale essential labels so nothing falls below `42 px`. Never expose the edge of the canvas.

## Sound

A warm, tactile 1980s coastal analog-synth and neon-foley world — punchy, lush, and rhythmic without harsh digital piercing. Every sound lands within 1 frame (`33 ms`) of its visible contact point, sharing the exact beat table clock.

- **Room & Music Bed:** Subtle coastal-night analog tape warmth (filtered brown noise `< 260 Hz`, `-36 dB`) plus an optional warm 104 BPM retro-coastal synth pulse: muted analog sawtooth bass (lowpass `520 Hz`) on 8th-note roots, lush detuned Juno-style pad chords with major-7th voicings (`cutoff 1100 Hz`, `-22 dB`), and a soft brushed electronic rim/shaker on offbeats.
- **Foley & Material Vocabulary (11 distinct voices):**
  1. *Neon tube ignite:* filtered 120 Hz sawtooth buzz bursts + soft `1.8 kHz` glass ping (`45 ms`, `-16 dB`), synced to perimeter trace start.
  2. *Interior light sweep:* warm bandpass pink noise sweeping `320 -> 1400 -> 600 Hz` over `0.35 s` (`-18 dB`).
  3. *Art Deco pattern lock:* crisp analog marimba/FM bell dyad (`ratio 2.0`, `< 1.8 kHz`, `-14 dB`), rising along a major pentatonic scale per card.
  4. *Portal dive whoosh:* deep warm air swell (`220 -> 780 Hz` pink noise) + subtle sub-sine drop (`95 -> 52 Hz`, `-13 dB`), peaking at mid-transition.
  5. *Connector rail trace:* silky filtered noise glide (`700 -> 1600 Hz`) matching exact draw duration (`-21 dB`), ending in a tight `1.2 kHz` diamond chime.
  6. *Counter tick:* soft analog woodblock/rim click (`1.4 kHz`, `18 ms`, `-18 dB`, capped at `<= 12/s`, skipping `80 ms` around landings).
  7. *Error stutter:* dissonant minor-second low synth stab (`E2 + F2`, lowpass `650 Hz`, `-12 dB`) + 3 quick neon flicker crackles (`2.2 kHz`, `12 ms`).
  8. *Calibration dial clicks:* 5–7 decelerating analog detent clicks (`1.9 kHz` + `180 Hz` body, `-15 dB`) timed to the dial curve.
  9. *Success chord:* warm rising 3-note synth brass/pluck triad (`-11 dB`).
  10. *Climax hit:* `0.30 s` music dropout, then a deep analog sub-drop (`62 -> 36 Hz`, `0.55 s`), wide 5-note major-9th synth chord, and shimmering glass chime — the single loudest moment in the mix.
  11. *Closing resolve:* warm tonic major-7th arpeggio fading smoothly across the `>= 1.2 s` final hold (zero audio in the final `50 ms`).
- **Mix & Delivery:** Pan foreground events by screen `x` (`(x / 1920 - 0.5) * 1.25`), max `3` simultaneous foreground hits, compressor threshold `-14 dB` (ratio `3:1`), integrated loudness `-19` to `-12` LUFS (target `-16` LUFS), sample peaks held `<= -4 dBFS` (so encoded true peaks stay `<= -1 dBFS`), zero hard clippers, zero silence gaps `> 1.5 s`.

## Hero frame

The final held frame (`>= 1.2 s` before end): matte `#1C212C` stage with fine film grain, the protagonist silhouette (`>= 360 px` tall) centered with a glowing golden-hour double-exposure interior and crisp `#FFF3E0` rim light, flanked symmetrically by active Art Deco mini-plaques and house animals rendered as miniature Deco silhouettes in the lower corners, topped by the brief's closing headline/number in heavy white condensed type with magenta bloom and coral script eyebrow. Reads unmistakably as **Palma Deco** with zero caption needed.

## Style check

Never ship a stub or "similar for the rest": every person, item, and step in the brief must be drawn with distinct silhouettes and patterns. Inspect frames at `0%`, `10%`, `25%`, `50%`, `75%`, and `95%` of duration:

- [ ] Frame `0` is already lit, composed, and readable (zero blank or fading-from-black opener).
- [ ] All three identity signatures (clipped Art Deco / double-exposure silhouettes, bipartite neon rim + 2 px edge highlight, two-voice coral-script + condensed-white bloom typography) appear in `>= 80%` of frames.
- [ ] Stage is matte `#1C212C` slate (never pure `#000000`), with active negative-space cutouts inside complex silhouettes.
- [ ] Primary figures `>= 1/3` frame height (`>= 360 px`) with `>= 50 px` faces; `2–4` cards per view with distinct pictorial actions for every task required by the brief; no face `< 24 px` in reunion.
- [ ] Text at `1920x1080`: titles `>= 66 px`, essential labels `>= 42 px`, key numbers `>= 96 px`; zero overlap or clipping.
- [ ] Continuous motion: `<= 15%` identical consecutive frames; every element enters (`>= 0.3 s`), lives, and exits smoothly; final hero frame holds `>= 1.2 s`.
- [ ] `renderAudio()` outputs `48000 Hz` stereo within `-19` to `-12` LUFS, sample peaks `<= -4 dBFS`, zero silence `> 1.5 s`, and one clear loudest climax hit.


## Production notes for a narrative film

Draw each task as an observable action inside its station. Derive your own array of subjects, silhouettes and gesture functions from the brief, keep each local action deterministic and give it a genuinely different object or composition. Build the previously occluded area of every plate before animating its cover. In the reunion, place local details where the camera can show them again. Beyond the slate border, internal illumination should change with the physical surfaces. Leave enough quiet slate to read the names against it.

A short film needs optical continuity between events. Select a repeating visible anchor such as a cut corner, profile or incised monogram, then line up the two scenes on that anchor. Move through the opening when its colored rim reaches the sides of the frame. A new layout must inherit at least one shape or direction of motion from the preceding shot. Treat the penultimate gathering and last image as separate beats. Keep brief-specific writing out of the reusable style file and supply it when staging the film.

The following drawing and production notes apply inside the illustrated plates. Retain the five color families, stage, typography and rhythmic accents in the accompanying design rules. The following sections give rules for movement, depth and recognizability when the brief calls for an action outside the demonstrations.

### Drawing objects inside the aperture

Establish a line of action, an articulated head or leading joint and the area carrying the weight. Follow the anatomy of a wing from shoulder coverts into secondaries and the long tips of primaries. Put a short unlit edge along the underside of each overlapping tier. Change the direction and length of incisions at a joint. Leave a reserve of key ink around the eye. A long thin leg needs its own contrast against both water and the light column. Test all thin structures at a reduced viewing size.

**Architecture.** Build a base, inhabited bays and a crown with a discernible construction logic. A small Deco rhythm can be expressed by a stepped parapet, three shallow horizontal fins or a gently curved corner. Use one of these strongly. Make the occluding foreground jamb thicker than the mullion inside the exposure. Put a contact shadow under the fins and a lighter interrupted edge only along their tops. Keep window interiors cooler than sun-warmed plaster and reserve a few reflected panes for the visual path.

**Botanical engraving.** Design a frond with a weight-bearing midrib, a different sweep on either side and alternating lengths of pinnae. Each leaflet tapers and droops from its own attachment. Let neighboring leaves occasionally overlap and cast a separate cool wedge. Group smaller leaves in pairs or masses at distance. Fold a fan leaf along oblique ribs and give alternating facets noticeably different values. Stop hatching before it reaches the outermost ragged edge. It is useful to leave one leaf whole against the sky as a scale cue.

**Water and reflections.** Project the source down into the water plane. Spread reflections toward the observer. Break strips where a mooring, boat or shadow interrupts them. Lay broader, calmer pigment bands horizontally in the distance. Make foreground marks wider or longer but less numerous per unit of apparent area. Draw the underside of a hull first, the reflected patch next and the hard gunwale last. A single bright seam can separate a small boat from a busy quay.

**Figure and clothing.** Draw the weight-bearing side of the pelvis and the compressed or stretched side of the rib cage. Place a real elbow break and shift the outer line to the far shoulder. Put a silhouette of the face against a clear patch with enough room for the brow, nose, lips and chin. For a focused figure use at least a third of the frame height and a face about 50 pixels or larger at full HD. Large casts travel through the frame in small groups; when gathered, keep faces above 24 pixels if they must be read. Limit texture in skin shadows. Place directional folds on cuffs and seams before introducing any atmospheric double exposure.

**Objects and machinery.** Anchor the perspective of all hard edges to a common ground plane. Use a few incisive changes of tangent to communicate the specific object, then soften the remaining edges with pigment. A handwheel needs spokes entering a central hub, a visible outer rim and a cast shadow at its mounting. A lever needs a pivot, a shaft and a grip. Let the orientation of those elements visually explain how the object is used. A merely glowing capsule conveys little mechanical information.

**Counts and lists.** Derive the actual number of marks from the supplied content. Represent a small list as a line of individually exposed windows or labels cut into an architectural band. Bring a large collection in as several groups and preserve an indication of how many groups remain. Use type only where the viewer needs to distinguish specific items. Interrupting the opening with a near leaf is acceptable only after the label has had time to be read.

**Letters and numerals.** For a short display title, choose a locally available condensed grotesque or draw custom lettering with grounded verticals, clipped counters and an occasional reversed curve. For editorial emphasis use an italic serif with distinct thick and thin strokes. Give small interface text clean system sans letterforms with little tracking. Use a warm solid text surface or an ink shadow immediately behind it. Keep the bright contour of a subject from touching the ascenders. A field of outlined, tilted words can rarely carry more than one short phrase.

### Crossing the registration plane

Treat outline geometry and interior painting as separate coordinate systems. Compute the outline transform at time t, establish the clip, then draw a translated and scaled interior using its own world-to-view transform. Restore before drawing the exterior ink and the few feather, plating or stone edges that actually lie above the opening. When the aperture encloses the frame, its outer rim may travel out of view. Keep the inner scenery continuous across that instant. Test both sides of the switch with single frames and at playback speed.

**Eclipse by structure.** Anchor a column, shutter or long exterior leaf close to the lens. Make the width sufficient to physically cover the seam between two scenes. Accelerate smoothly toward the occlusion, change the plate while that area is covered, and emerge moving in the same direction. Set the duration from the actual screen coverage, not from a universal wipe timer. Design two reveal windows if the near object has perforations; the outgoing scene may still be visible in one while the incoming starts to appear in the other.

**Register and light.** Begin with an ink recess and two thin offsets of color, with the same underlying outline. As the print seats, align the offsets and reveal an interior image from one side. Release a very short cut of paper along the edge as pressure settles. This works for an emblem, icon or confirmation in a component. Use an original silhouette with a counter or incision engineered into its shape. The action must still be intelligible at reduced color saturation.

### Optical checks on the camera

Define a world larger than its final view and include overscan on every moving plate. The furthest atmosphere might take 3 to 7 percent of nominal travel, far architecture 16 to 25 percent, middle subject 35 to 55 percent, and a near screen 90 to 135 percent. Choose values that reveal actual information rather than chasing ratios for their own sake. Move the camera origin around a visual anchor when zooming. If the zoom targets an aperture, place the origin inside its thickest region so it does not accidentally escape through empty background.

For an oblique push add mild pitch or a change of lens centering on the near plate only if the apparent geometry supports it. Building verticals should stay coherent. Preserve a believable relationship between the advancing film plane and the horizon. Let a near object pass close enough to show its carved ridges. Keep the horizon gently moving behind the mask. Avoid moving the sun a large distance relative to the very remote cloud band. An extreme difference between perceived distances already provides drama without additional shakes.

Judge one frame early, one at contact and one after the camera has passed through the aperture. Find a point on each plane and track it relative to its neighbors. Something that was genuinely covered must become visible. Check two adjacent frames on the most rapid move. If a black border, unpainted canvas edge or single-frame changing mask is visible, extend the paintings, match transforms and rerender. Do not paper over the error with a full-frame flash.

### Legibility at delivery size

The best cover usually comes just before the silhouette begins to occupy the entire view: its outline is still recognizable and there is enough interior detail to reward inspection. Another valid cover is a newly revealed vista framed by a real foreground object. Inspect both at gallery size. Choose the frame in which important edges remain separated and the light shapes read as a small group rather than a field of specks. Never infer quality from the number of generated paths or the duration of a render.

Review with the palette reduced to luminance, at small size, and with textured layers temporarily hidden. Confirm the silhouette reads in all three conditions. Play the full shot at normal speed once while watching the near edge and again while watching the distant horizon. The near edge should conceal and reveal parallax; the horizon should feel grounded. Check a stationary point on the subject for grain crawling, especially after video compression. Render a clean frame beyond both extremes of travel before final cropping.

## House animals

Two recurring animals live in the background of every film that has room for animal life. They are not part of the story: they never act out a step of the brief, never carry a caption, and never take focus from the brief's characters. They are the film's running gag and its signature. Draw them in the medium of the style (ink, paint, print, clay, pixels), but keep their shapes, proportions and behavior exactly as written here, so they read as the same two animals in every style. They replace any generic background animal the style might otherwise use (birds, pets, lizards).

Proportions are given in **head units (h)**: the height of that animal's head.

### The capybara

Calm, round, unbothered. The world can explode around it; it keeps sitting.

- **Body:** one soft barrel, a rounded rectangle 3.4h long and 2.0h tall, with the back as one smooth arc slightly higher at the hips than at the shoulders. No visible waist, no neck: the head joins the body directly.
- **Head:** a large blunt block 1.6h long and 1.0h tall, with rounded corners. The muzzle is deep and square: the front of the face is an almost vertical flat plane, the top of the head an almost straight line from the ears to the nose.
- **Nose:** two short nostril slits high on the front plane, near the top corner. A soft vertical cleft runs down to a small closed mouth.
- **Eyes:** small (0.14h), set high, near the top line of the head and about one third back from the nose. Always relaxed: half-closed, lids as one gentle downward curve. They open fully only once, at the film's single biggest impact, and close again.
- **Ears:** two small round ears (0.22h) set far back and high, slightly cupped.
- **Legs:** four short straight legs, 0.6h tall, mostly hidden under the belly when it sits. Front feet with four short toes, back feet with three, each toe a small rounded wedge.
- **Tail:** none.
- **Coat:** warm reddish brown, slightly lighter on the belly and under the jaw, darker along the spine. Coarse: a few short dry strokes or texture marks along the back, never fluffy.
- **Size:** about 0.55 of an adult human's height at the shoulder, so it reads clearly next to people.
- **Behavior:** sits or lies like a loaf, chewing slowly (jaw moves 2 times per second). Things land on its head (a fruit, a leaf, a falling paper, a small object from the story's world) and it does not react, except for one slow blink. It moves at most twice in the film, at a calm walk.
- **Sound:** a soft low huff (pink noise, lowpass 400 Hz, 180 ms) when something lands on it; gentle chewing clicks (filtered noise, 1.2 kHz, 15 ms, 2 per second) at very low level when the camera is close. Never squeaks.

### The sloth

Slow, wide-eyed, always one beat behind the rest of the world.

- **Body:** a shaggy pear, 2.2h tall, hanging or clinging, never walking upright. Arms are very long (2.6h), legs shorter (1.6h), each limb ending in three long curved claws (0.5h), slightly darker than the coat.
- **Neck:** long and slightly arched, 0.9h, so the head can swing and turn far from the body; it bends like a hook when the sloth hangs upside down and looks at the camera.
- **Head:** small and round, slightly wider than tall (1.1h wide, 1.0h tall), with a short flat face.
- **Eyes:** big and bulging, each 0.32h, two round domes set high on the head and slightly apart, with small black pupils. The eyes are the joke: in surprise they swell to 0.38h and the pupils shrink to dots; when confused, each pupil looks a slightly different way.
- **Face marks:** a pale face disc with a dark mask stripe running back from each eye toward the ear, like a three-toed sloth. This mask is the sloth's identity mark and must always be visible.
- **Nose and mouth:** a small dark oval nose; a gentle closed smile. No teeth showing, no overbite.
- **Coat:** long, shaggy, greyish beige with a faint green tint (algae), lighter on the face. Hair hangs downward from the limbs with gravity.
- **Size:** about 0.45 of an adult human's height when hanging stretched.
- **Behavior:** hangs from whatever is high in the scene (a wire, a branch, a lamp, a frame, a shelf). Moves at one quarter of the speed of everything else. Reacts to every big event about 0.8 s late: when everyone has already moved on, the sloth slowly turns its head, eyes swelling. Once in the film, it reaches for something that is no longer there.
- **Sound:** a slow wooden creak of the thing it hangs from (bandpass noise 300 to 500 Hz, 400 ms) when it moves; one long soft yawn (sine 260 to 180 Hz through a 700 Hz formant, 0.9 s) late in the film. Never speaks.

### Rules

- Both are original animals, not any existing cartoon character. No buck teeth, no overbite, no lisp, no clothes, no human hands, no speech.
- Both appear in at least three moments of the film, one of them the final image (the capybara sitting at the edge of the frame, the sloth hanging above it), small enough never to compete with the brief's characters.
- If the style has no place for animals (pure typography, abstract diagrams), leave them out.

## Content

Tell the film what to show: the subject, the main character, the steps or items to explain, any numbers, and the closing line. One idea that fits 10 to 16 seconds. If something is missing, choose it yourself and do not ask.

## Technical delivery

Deliver one complete, self-contained HTML file. Do not ask for approval and do not send a storyboard first: go straight to the finished film.

- Picture is drawn on one `<canvas>` of 1920x1080 at 30 frames per second, scaled to fit the browser window.
- All sound is synthesized with the Web Audio API. No image, video or audio files. The only thing that may load from the network is Google Fonts.
- A single Play button starts picture and sound together. It can be pressed again to replay.
- Every frame is a pure function of time, so any instant can be drawn in any order. All randomness comes from a seeded generator: never use `Math.random`, `Date` or `performance.now` inside drawing or sound scheduling.
- The final mix should measure around -16 LUFS with true peaks below -1 dBFS in the exported video. Encoding the audio adds up to 3.5 dB of overshoot, so the last stage of the mix must hold sample peaks at or below -4 dBFS. Use gain riding and compression to reach the loudness; never use a hard clipper.

## Continuous motion (applies to every style)

The point of a film made in code is that nothing jumps. Every frame is computed, so every change can be gradual.

- **Every property moves a little on every frame**, at 30 frames per second: position, size, rotation, opacity, color, perspective, shadow, blur, line length, fill level. Interpolate from the previous state with an easing curve; never set a new value from one frame to the next. Moving from x to x + 30 takes many frames (x + 1, x + 2, x + 3...), not one.
- **Every element has a beginning, a middle and an end.** It enters with a visible motion of at least 0.3 s (grows from a point, slides in, draws on, unfolds, is carried in, rises into focus), with an ease-out and a small overshoot. While on screen it keeps a secondary motion (breathing, drift, sway, idle, shimmer). It leaves with a visible motion (shrinks, slides out, is covered, falls away, fades into the background). Nothing appears or vanishes on a single frame.
- **Depth and light move too.** Things coming closer grow, sharpen and gain contrast; things moving away shrink and fade toward the background color. Shadows follow their objects and stretch with the light. The camera moves in perspective, with near layers moving faster than far ones.
- **Changes of place are continuous camera or world moves** (push in, pull back, pan, crane, a physical object covering the frame) lasting 0.4 to 1 s. A hard cut only marks a jump in time, at most once per film.
- **The frame is never still.** Even during a hold, something moves: idle breathing, a drifting particle, a slow camera creep. No more than 15% of frames may be identical to the previous one.
- **Stepped motion only on purpose.** If the style asks for animation on twos or pixel steps, the steps are small and regular along a continuous path; it is never a jump to a distant position.
- **Before delivering**, step through consecutive frames at the busiest moments. Fix any element that moves more than about 48 px between two frames without motion blur or a smear, and any element that appears or disappears from one frame to the next.

Expose this object so the film can be exported to video:

```js
window.film = {
  canvas,            // the canvas element
  width: 1920,
  height: 1080,
  fps: 30,
  duration,          // total seconds
  ready,             // Promise that resolves when fonts and pre-rendered layers are ready
  seek(seconds),     // draws exactly that instant on the canvas
  renderAudio(),     // Promise<AudioBuffer>: the whole soundtrack, stereo, rendered in an
                     // OfflineAudioContext by the same code that plays it live
}
```
